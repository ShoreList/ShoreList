Water safety and rules

Know before you go

The few things worth knowing before you swim, surf or paddle at Wrightsville Beach, Carolina Beach and Kure Beach, with links to the agencies that set the rules.

Last reviewed November 2026. Rules change; confirm with the official source.

The short version

  • Swim near a lifeguard and obey the flags posted on the beach. They come before any app, including ours.
  • Learn how to spot a rip current and how to get out of one.
  • On a paddleboard or kayak, have a US Coast Guard approved life jacket for every person, and a whistle.
  • In North Carolina, anyone under 13 must wear a life jacket on a recreational vessel that is underway.

Lifeguards and flags come first

Lifeguards are on duty seasonally at many beach accesses. Swim near a lifeguard, obey the flags posted on the beach, and never swim alone. If you are unsure what a flag means or whether it is a good day to go in, ask the lifeguard. That is what they are there for.

ShoreList shows live surf conditions and an estimated flag on the surf check and the weather page. Those are estimates from wave, wind and weather data. They are not official lifeguard flags, and the flags and lifeguards on the beach always come first.

Rip current basics

A rip current is a narrow channel of water moving away from the shore. Rip currents can form on any beach with breaking waves, on calm sunny days as well as rough ones.

How to spot one

  • A gap in the line of breaking waves, where the water looks deceptively calm.
  • A strip of churning, choppy or differently colored water.
  • Foam, seaweed or debris moving steadily out to sea.

They are not always easy to see from the sand. When in doubt, ask a lifeguard before you get in.

If you are caught in one

  • Stay calm. A rip current pulls you away from shore. It does not pull you under.
  • Do not swim straight back against it. That is how swimmers tire.
  • Swim parallel to the beach until you are out of the current, then angle back toward shore.
  • If you cannot swim out of it, float or tread water, and wave and call for help.

If you see someone in trouble

Get a lifeguard. If there is no lifeguard, call 911. Throw the person something that floats and call out instructions from shore. Going in after them puts you in the same current.

Life jackets on paddleboards, kayaks and canoes

This is where visitors are most often surprised. Here is what the two agencies say on the pages we read, in plain language.

US Coast Guard

  • A stand up paddleboard counts as a vessel when it is used beyond the narrow limits of a swimming, surfing or bathing area. Once it is a vessel, the paddler must carry a life jacket.
  • Federal law calls for a Coast Guard approved, wearable life jacket, in good and serviceable condition, for each person aboard.
  • A life jacket must be Coast Guard approved to count toward that requirement.
  • The Coast Guard says inflatable life jackets are not for weak swimmers or non-swimmers, and it sets a minimum age for an inflatable to count. Read its page before relying on one.

US Coast Guard: wearing your approved life jacket

North Carolina Wildlife Resources Commission

  • North Carolina requires anyone under 13 to wear an appropriate life jacket when on a recreational vessel that is underway.
  • Each person aboard needs a life jacket of an approved type, in good condition, of the right size and within reach.
  • The agency says this covers canoes, kayaks, rowboats and other non-motorized craft.
  • Anyone riding a personal watercraft such as a jet ski, or being towed by one, must wear a Coast Guard approved life jacket.

The North Carolina pages we read do not mention paddleboards by name. The Coast Guard page does. The simple approach is to treat a paddleboard like any other small craft: have a life jacket for everyone, and wear it.

Carry a whistle

The Coast Guard requires vessels under 39.4 feet to have a way of making an efficient sound signal. That includes a kayak, or a paddleboard being used as a vessel. A whistle qualifies. The Coast Guard says a human voice does not. Clip one to your life jacket and it is always with you.

Registration

According to the NC Wildlife Resources Commission, rowboats, canoes, kayaks and rafts moved only by oars, paddles or the current do not need to be registered. All motorized vessels must be registered. If you add a motor of any kind to a small craft, check the registration page before you launch.

What gear is, and is not

  • A surfboard or paddleboard leash keeps the board near you. It is not a flotation or rescue device.
  • Pool floats, swim aids and impact vests are not life jackets. Look for the Coast Guard approval label.
  • A life jacket only helps if it fits and you are wearing it.
  • Nothing sold in the ShoreList shop is a life jacket or a safety device.

Before you leave the sand

  • Check the conditions and the tide, then check the flags when you arrive.
  • Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to be back.
  • Go with a friend, especially when paddling.
  • On a rental, a lesson or a tour, follow your operator's briefing. They know the water you are on.
  • Bring water and sun protection, and get out before you are tired.
  • If you hear thunder or see lightning, get off the water and off the beach.
