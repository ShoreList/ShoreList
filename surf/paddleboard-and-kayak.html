Buyer's guide

Paddleboards and kayaks: inflatable or hard

The best one is the one you will actually take out. That depends less on the water than on your car, your storage and how you like to spend a morning.

CompareCarrying and storingLocal waterTry first

The short version

  • Inflatables win on transport and storage. They travel in a bag and live in a closet.
  • Hard boards and hard kayaks win on glide, tracking and feel, and they are ready the moment you reach the water.
  • For calm, protected water and occasional use, an inflatable is a sensible first choice.
  • Rent or join a guided paddle first. Tides and wind matter more here than the craft does.

How they compare

Inflatable

Packs down, travels anywhere

  • Rolls into a bag that fits in a car trunk or a closet.
  • Needs pumping up before you launch and drying before you pack it away.
  • Tough against bumps and scrapes that would ding a hard board.
  • Sits a little higher and flexes a little more, so it can feel slower and catch more wind.
Hard

Better on the water, bulkier off it

  • Stiffer, so it glides farther with each stroke and holds a straighter line.
  • Nothing to inflate. Carry it down and go.
  • Needs a roof rack or a truck bed, and a garage, shed or wall to store it.
  • Hard paddleboards can ding. Hard plastic kayaks are heavy but take a lot of knocks.

Paddleboards

A wider, thicker board is more stable. A longer, narrower one is faster and tracks straighter but is easier to fall off. For a first board on flat water, choose stability. With an inflatable, pump it to the pressure its maker recommends. An under-inflated board bends in the middle and is hard work to paddle.

Kayaks

Sit-on-top kayaks are open, stable and easy to climb back onto, which is why most guided tours here use them. Sit-inside kayaks keep you drier and are more efficient over distance, and they take more practice. Inflatable kayaks follow the same trade as inflatable boards: easy to move and store, a little slower and more affected by wind.

Carrying and storing

Be realistic about this part. It decides how often you go.

  • Your car. A hard board or kayak travels on a roof rack, in a truck bed or on a trailer, and has to be tied down properly every time. An inflatable goes in the trunk.
  • Your home. Hard craft need wall, ceiling or floor space out of direct sun. An inflatable needs a shelf, and it should be dry before it is stored.
  • The walk to the water. Hard kayaks are heavy and awkward for one person. Check you can carry what you buy from the parking space to the launch.
  • Time. Inflating takes a few minutes of real effort. Loading and strapping a hard craft takes its own time. Neither is instant.
  • Heat. Do not leave any board or kayak baking in a closed car or in full sun for long. Follow the maker's care instructions.

Wind is the thing beginners underestimate. A breeze that feels pleasant on the beach can make the paddle home slow, especially on an inflatable. Head out into the wind so that it helps you on the way back, and check the tide before you launch.

Life jackets and whistles are part of the kit. There are federal and North Carolina rules for paddlers. We summarize them, with links to the official sources, in Know before you go.

Try before you buy

A rental or a guided paddle lets you feel the difference between a wide board and a narrow one, or a sit-on-top and a sit-inside, without owning either. Guides also teach the local part: where the current runs, when the tide turns and which way to go when the wind picks up.

ShoreList does not sell paddleboards or kayaks. When you are ready to buy, a local shop can match a craft to your car, your storage and the water you plan to paddle.
